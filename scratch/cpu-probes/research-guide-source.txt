# Understanding Strategic Learning in Manabot

*A research guide informed by Ataraxos — October 2026*

**Context:** [Fantasia / Manabot](https://github.com/loopflowstudio/fantasia)  
**Primary paper:** Sokota et al., [*Scalable decision-making for games of imperfect information*](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11036-y), *Nature* (2026).

## Purpose

This is a **scientific guide, not an implementation plan**. It explains the research strategy, underlying mathematics, hypotheses, and experiments that would help us understand how strong, durable strategic behavior emerges from self-play in imperfect-information MTG. Future agents should turn these questions into concrete scoped plans only after inspecting the then-current code and research evidence.

The five connected questions are: **(1)** What are we regularizing against and why? **(2)** How should learning trajectories balance exploration and exploitation, including possible mini-cycles? **(3)** How do we recognize strategic cycling rather than durable progress? **(4)** How can Managym generate useful training and search evidence more efficiently, particularly before CUDA? **(5)** How should search improve a trained policy, starting with a single magnetic mirror descent (MMD) update? Advantage filtering, belief calibration, and information-safe continuation policies belong within this picture.

## The underlying scientific problem

In a stationary single-agent task, learning often resembles hill-climbing. In a competitive imperfect-information game, **changing your strategy changes the environment in which your strategy is evaluated**. Bluffing works because aggression sometimes indicates genuine strength; changing bluff frequency changes how opponents respond to both bluffs and genuine aggression. In two-player zero-sum notation, the payoff \(u(x,y)\) depends on *both* players' mixed strategies. Optimizing against a moving opponent \(y_t\) is not optimizing a fixed objective. Even simple matrix games can exhibit cycling under simultaneous gradient updates.

Figure 1 of Ataraxos illustrates this elementary game-theoretic fact. Its novelty is not the observation itself, but the question of whether relatively conventional deep RL can be made stable and effective despite it.

We must distinguish **optimization progress** (loss, KL, entropy), **relative progress** (wins against the latest opponent), and **durable strategic progress** (strength across historical, diverse, and held-out opponents). The first two can improve while the third worsens.

### What Ataraxos proves—and does not

Ataraxos reports 15 wins, 1 loss, and 4 draws against champion Pim Niemeijer, approximately 163 million self-play games and 208 billion environment steps, and a historical training-rental estimate under $8,000. Relative to DeepNash, it reports roughly 1/500 the compute cost, 1/30 the games, and 1/100 the training examples. Those are **system-level** outcomes, not evidence that any single component explains the gap. DeepNash (2022) was not pre-PPO (2017). The system combines regularized self-play, architecture choices, advantage filtering, belief learning, test-time rollouts, MMD, and a CUDA simulator. Its ablations test components within a tuned recipe; they do not conclusively isolate the special value of *coordinating* annealing schedules. The search concept builds on the authors' 2024 update-equivalence paper; determinization and KL policy updates are older.

# I. Regularization: what distribution, for what purpose?

The most important distinction from our discussion is that similar-looking KL penalties can have **different meanings**.

| Mechanism | Compared against | Scientific role |
|---|---|---|
| Update KL | Previous/data-collection policy | Limit abrupt strategic change |
| Fixed-reference KL | Deliberately chosen action prior | Shape the policy and preserve strategic diversity |
| Search trust KL | Raw trained policy | Prevent noisy search from overruling learned knowledge |
| Search magnetic KL | Fixed action prior | Preserve the regularized game objective during local search |

In Ataraxos, the move-policy fixed reference selects a **movable piece uniformly, then one of its legal moves uniformly**. This is not uniform over all moves. The separate setup network uses maximum-entropy regularization. The old-policy and fixed-reference terms both appear at inference time, but the paper's striking ablation—search becoming worse than no search when KL is removed—specifically concerns **KL to the trained move network**, not an isolated ablation of the fixed random reference.

## Why KL and mirror descent appear

Ordinary gradient descent is equivalent to optimizing a local linear approximation while penalizing squared Euclidean movement:

\[
x_{t+1}=\arg\min_x\left[\langle\nabla f(x_t),x-x_t\rangle+\frac{1}{2\eta}\|x-x_t\|_2^2\right].
\]

Mirror descent replaces Euclidean distance with a Bregman divergence generated by a convex mirror map \(\psi\):

\[
D_\psi(p,q)=\psi(p)-\psi(q)-\langle\nabla\psi(q),p-q\rangle.
\]

Choosing negative entropy, \(\psi(p)=\sum_a p(a)\log p(a)\), yields \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q)\) on the probability simplex. For categorical action values \(Q(a)\), the KL mirror update is

\[
\pi_{t+1}(a)\propto\pi_t(a)e^{\eta Q(a)}.
\]

It changes probabilities multiplicatively and naturally preserves the simplex. KL is asymmetric and not a metric, but it is a natural distributional geometry, locally related to Fisher information and natural gradients. **A KL penalty added to an Adam-optimized neural loss is not itself mirror descent.** PPO clipping, trust-region KL, natural gradients, and tabular mirror descent all control related quantities in different ways. PPO clipping is a surrogate-objective mechanism, not a hard limit on final policy KL.

A schematic regularized improvement objective is

\[
\max_\pi\sum_a\pi(a)Q(a)-\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{\mathrm{old}})-\alpha D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\rho).
\]

The old-policy term controls **movement**; the fixed prior \(\rho\) shapes **where the policy is pulled**. This is schematic, not the full Ataraxos neural loss.

## Why MTG complicates the reference prior

Uniform over encoded legal actions is not necessarily neutral: a spell with dozens of targeting or payment encodings may receive far more probability than a strategically comparable action with one encoding. Candidate priors include uniform over encoded actions, hierarchical priors over action families and parameters, scripted priors, or frozen learned priors. Each encodes assumptions. The prior's support matters too: under \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\rho)\), \(\rho(a)=0\) prohibits positive \(\pi(a)\).

**Training exploration, strategically necessary equilibrium mixing, and entropy among redundant encodings are distinct.** A strong final MTG policy may be nearly deterministic at some information states and intentionally stochastic at bluff-sensitive ones.

### What to understand and test

We need a precise account of the *actual* Manabot loss and optimizer—do not assume PPO without inspecting the code—and of which mechanisms act on parameters, sampled ratios, or whole action distributions. Measure old/new policy KL, reference KL, entropy by decision family and legal-action count, clip fraction, gradient norms, and policy changes on fixed MTG situations. Compare old-policy KL, fixed-reference KL, entropy bonuses, and structured priors separately and together, with matched compute and opponents. Ask whether gains improve **held-out robustness** rather than merely smoothing training.

# II. Training trajectories: global annealing and possible mini-cycles

Ataraxos uses stronger regularization and larger updates early, weaker regularization and smaller updates later. Early updates can discover useful behavior rapidly while regularization damps instability; later smaller updates refine the strategy without destroying it. Explore-early/exploit-late and learning-rate annealing are conventional. The stronger Ataraxos claim is that **regularization strength and update magnitude should be coordinated** in a moving strategic environment. Its ablations support the chosen schedules but do not conclusively show that coordination beats independently retuned schedules.

## Three timescales

1. **Global progression:** broad discovery and relatively large updates early, then refinement.
2. **Intermediate exploration–exploitation mini-cycles:** deliberately reopen exploration after a plateau or strategic collapse, then refine again. This is a plausible *Manabot hypothesis*, not an Ataraxos result; it could also destroy useful learning.
3. **Persistent strategic mixing:** randomness retained because deterministic behavior is exploitable. This is not temporary training exploration.

A small Adam step can still produce a large strategic change. Distinguish parameter movement, policy movement, and behavior movement:

\[
\|\theta_{t+1}-\theta_t\|,\quad
\mathbb E_I D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{t+1}(\cdot\mid I)\|\pi_t(\cdot\mid I)),\quad
\Delta\text{behavior on fixed decision probes}.
\]

The last quantity asks whether Manabot actually changed how it attacks into open mana, bluffs, blocks, or conserves resources.

A reward plateau might indicate a strong equilibrium against current opponents, a weak local attractor, insufficient exploration, a representation limit, or noisy measurement. An entropy decline might indicate premature collapse **or** genuinely learned decisive play. Neither is diagnostic by itself.

### What to understand and test

Do distinct strategic phases emerge? Does entropy collapse precede stagnation? Does coupling schedules matter beyond good independent tuning? Do cyclic schedules or opponent-population changes recover lost strategic diversity? Compare monotonic, independently tuned, coordinated, adaptive, and eventually cyclic schedules under matched budgets and seeds. Predeclare triggers for adaptive interventions; evaluate against frozen opponents and strategic probes, not just the latest self-play opponent. Treat cyclic exploration as a research hypothesis until supported.

# III. Strategic cycling: what counts as durable improvement?

Separate **nontransitivity** (A beats B, B beats C, C beats A), **temporal cycling** (training revisits earlier behaviors), and **exploitability** (vulnerability to a best response). Nontransitivity can coexist with a stable mixed equilibrium. A finite opponent matrix is an empirical robustness measure, not exact exploitability for full MTG.

For frozen checkpoints \(P_i\), define the empirical payoff matrix

\[
M_{ij}=\mathbb E[\text{effective outcome of }P_i\text{ against }P_j].
\]

A policy may beat its predecessor and lose to a much older checkpoint. Evaluate a time-ordered cohort against one another and against frozen anchors, controlling deck suite, seats, paired deals, world/content versions, inference compute, and uncertainty. Fantasia already documents a world-pinned arena and frozen anchors; use that conceptual infrastructure rather than an isolated new Elo calculation.

A matrix reveals *who beats whom*, not *how strategies changed*. Complement it with a fixed suite of interpretable MTG decision states and compare full action distributions. Look for recurring behavioral signatures, regressions against old opponents, abrupt KL changes, and shifts in action-family frequencies. These are **signals**, not proof of cycling. An alert should require a practically meaningful and statistically supported historical regression, ideally despite improvement against recent opponents.

### What to understand and test

Do checkpoint policies improve across a diverse historical population? Are recurring modes observable independently of Elo? Does historical-opponent sampling stabilize learning? Can we distinguish healthy strategic mixing from optimizer oscillation? Compare latest-policy self-play with population-based self-play while keeping the rest of the system fixed. Record confidence intervals and negative results, including when apparent cycles disappear with more evaluation data.

# IV. Managym: optimize useful simulation, not just raw transitions

## What the code suggests

Our inspection of [Fantasia](https://github.com/loopflowstudio/fantasia) found several GPU-friendly *ideas* already in the Rust architecture: typed integer object IDs, indexed card/permanent storage, explicit effect resolution, an undo journal, vector environments, and rollout pools. Relevant entry points include [`game.rs`](https://github.com/loopflowstudio/fantasia/blob/main/managym/src/flow/game.rs), [`game_object.rs`](https://github.com/loopflowstudio/fantasia/blob/main/managym/src/state/game_object.rs), [`resolution.rs`](https://github.com/loopflowstudio/fantasia/blob/main/managym/src/flow/resolution.rs), [`undo.rs`](https://github.com/loopflowstudio/fantasia/blob/main/managym/src/flow/undo.rs), [`vector_env.rs`](https://github.com/loopflowstudio/fantasia/blob/main/managym/src/agent/vector_env.rs), and [`rollout_pool.rs`](https://github.com/loopflowstudio/fantasia/blob/main/managym/src/agent/rollout_pool.rs).

The inspected `VectorEnv::step` loops through environments sequentially; a batched API is not necessarily parallel execution. `RolloutPool::from_game` clones worlds and per-action rollouts; this creates a concrete place to compare cloning with undo-based branching. The engine still uses dynamic `Vec`s, maps, queues, and host-side Rust logic. **Compact indexed structures are helpful but do not make existing Rust code directly executable as CUDA kernels.** Divergent effect resolution, memory layout, synchronization, and preserving rules semantics remain significant issues.

## The more transferable Ataraxos systems insight

Ataraxos integrates its simulator and rollout buffer, preallocates storage, reconstructs observations and legal-action masks on demand, and supports fast resets to nonterminal states. The paper discusses a target around ten million board updates per second. It does **not** supply a controlled same-algorithm CPU-vs-CUDA speedup, so the roughly 500-fold historical training-cost comparison with DeepNash cannot be attributed to CUDA alone.

The goal for Manabot is **durable playing-strength improvement per unit of total compute**, not maximum raw game transitions per second. Simulation, observation encoding, policy inference, Python/Rust boundaries, rollout storage, and training all compete for time. Amdahl's law gives an immediate constraint: if fraction \(f\) of total time is simulation and that part becomes \(s\) times faster, the overall speedup is

\[
S=\frac{1}{(1-f)+f/s}.
\]

Even a spectacular GPU kernel cannot deliver a spectacular end-to-end speedup if neural inference or data movement dominates.

## CPU optimizations worth understanding before CUDA

The research questions are whether independent games can run efficiently across CPU cores, whether observations and inference can be batched with less overhead, whether cloned search states can be replaced by mark/apply/rollback, whether historical observations can be reconstructed rather than retained, and whether a shared immutable card-content representation improves memory locality. Training and search should be profiled **separately**: training runs many unrelated games; search often runs many branches from a shared root and may have different memory and synchronization properties.

A GPU prototype could be credible for a restricted semantic card set because the effect system already represents behavior structurally. But a device backend must either interpret shared semantic programs or duplicate transition logic. The latter creates substantial correctness and maintenance debt. A shared IR plus CPU/GPU differential testing is attractive in principle, but it is an architectural hypothesis requiring validation.

### What to understand and test

Measure raw transitions/sec, end-to-end steps/sec, observation construction, inference utilization, clone cost, undo cost, peak memory per rollout, and search evaluations/sec as concurrency grows. Compare serial CPU, multicore CPU, and any restricted GPU prototype **at equal semantic coverage and end-to-end workload**. Include correctness tests across branches, triggers, hidden-state materialization, and deterministic replay. Only after a strong CPU baseline should a CUDA feasibility result drive a larger architectural decision.

# V. Policy and search: search as one additional learning step

## What Ataraxos does

At inference time, Ataraxos samples plausible hidden states from a learned belief model, applies each candidate root action, runs depth-limited continuations under the trained move policy, evaluates rollout leaves with the value network, averages action-value estimates, and performs **one tabular magnetic mirror descent update at the current information state**. It samples the chosen move from the updated distribution. This is Monte Carlo rollout search, not AlphaZero-style explicit expanding MCTS.

The central scientific insight is that **search should modify a strong learned policy, not discard it**. Its estimates are imperfect because of finite sampling, inaccurate beliefs, value errors, and continuation policies that may not describe the actual opponent.

## MMD and the two references

Write the conceptual local objective as

\[
\pi_{\mathrm{search}}=\arg\max_\pi\left[
\sum_a\pi(a)\hat Q(a)
-\frac{1}{\eta}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{\mathrm{network}})
-\alpha D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi\|\rho)
\right].
\]

Here \(\pi_{\mathrm{network}}\) is the **raw trained policy before search**, and \(\rho\) is the **fixed reference**, which in Stratego is the random piece-then-move policy. The first term rewards search-estimated value; the second protects learned behavior; the third maintains the regularized strategic objective. This is the rationale behind *update equivalence*: inference-time search resembles one additional local policy improvement step using the same kinds of regularization as self-play training. The neural network weights are not changed at inference.

For a single tabular step with these KL terms, the closed-form distribution is

\[
\pi_{\mathrm{search}}(a)\propto
\pi_{\mathrm{network}}(a)^{1/(1+\eta\alpha)}
\rho(a)^{\eta\alpha/(1+\eta\alpha)}
\exp\!\left(\frac{\eta\hat Q(a)}{1+\eta\alpha}\right).
\]

With \(\alpha=0\), this reduces to ordinary KL mirror descent:

\[
\pi_{\mathrm{search}}(a)\propto\pi_{\mathrm{network}}(a)e^{\eta\hat Q(a)}.
\]

The fixed-reference term is **not inherently required for Monte Carlo search**. Its motivation is preserving the regularized game objective, not simply limiting overfitting. Ataraxos reports that removing KL **toward the trained move network** causes search to perform worse than no search. It does not cleanly establish the independent empirical value of the fixed-reference term. For Manabot, KL-only and full magnetic updates are therefore distinct hypotheses worth comparing.

The \(\hat Q\) scale, temperature/step size, action masking, zero-probability support, and uncertainty matter greatly. If action values are noisy, increasing \(\eta\) can turn a small estimation error into a large behavioral shift. More rollouts may justify a larger step, but that is an empirical hypothesis, not an automatic rule.

## Imperfect information: avoid leaking hidden truth

Sampling a complete hidden world for simulation is not the same as allowing the policy to **observe** it. Ataraxos's continuation policy operates on the simulated acting player's information state, rather than using full hidden state as policy input. Action values are aggregated over sampled worlds **before** producing one root distribution. This avoids the obvious mistake of choosing a different root action depending on hidden cards the agent cannot see.

However, this does **not** solve arbitrary imperfect-information subgames. Learned beliefs can be miscalibrated against unfamiliar opponents; rollout policies can be weak; value functions can be wrong; and a one-step root improvement does not jointly optimize all future information sets. Naive perfect-information determinization can suffer from strategy fusion. Information-limited rollouts mitigate direct leakage but do not guarantee full information-set consistency.

Fantasia already has sampled-world flat Monte Carlo and determinized PUCT planning, plus typed possible-world and belief interfaces. This makes the root update a comparatively isolated experiment: **reuse the same worlds, rollouts, evaluator, and compute budget, changing only how search evidence is converted into a final action distribution**.

### What to understand and test

Compare raw policy, current search selection, KL-only mirror descent, and full MMD. Evaluate under matched sampled worlds, rollout budgets, evaluator versions, and inference compute. Measure held-out strength, sensitivity to belief error, search depth/rollout scaling, and changes in policy KL. Test whether additional search compute ever makes the policy **more confidently wrong**, and whether stronger trust regularization prevents that. An important long-term question is whether the trust strength should adapt to estimated search uncertainty.

# VI. Supporting questions that change the interpretation of the five themes

## Advantage filtering: which experiences actually teach the policy?

Ataraxos filters move-training examples by the magnitude of estimated advantage. The authors report approximately **2.5× faster iterations**, plus improved sample efficiency and asymptotic performance in their configuration. This is more than a systems trick: selecting training examples changes the learning distribution and potentially the kinds of decisions the network learns first.

MTG has many trivial or nearly forced decisions, but advantage magnitude is not identical to strategic importance. Noisy advantage estimates may select surprising mistakes rather than informative examples; filtering can bias learning or suppress subtle strategically necessary actions. The informative experiment is not simply “filter and see if it gets faster.” It is to compare **strength per environment step, per training example, and per wall-clock dollar**, while observing decision-type coverage, advantage-estimation noise, and robustness. Advantage filtering belongs in the study of how learning emerges because it changes the *curriculum of evidence* the policy receives.

## Belief quality is a first-class capability

Ataraxos trains a separate autoregressive belief model from self-play, where hidden ground truth is available. It models opponent piece identities conditioned on the player's information state. In MTG, analogous beliefs concern opponent hand contents, library uncertainty, and inferences from public actions.

Beliefs should be evaluated separately from search: calibration, predictive likelihood, constraint satisfaction, and generalization to opponents with different styles. A better belief model is not automatically a stronger player, and a strong search result under a bad belief can be brittle. Compare legal-world sampling, exact-range models where available, and learned beliefs with **identical search compute**. Test sensitivity to deliberately misspecified beliefs. The research question is how much of search strength comes from correct uncertainty modeling rather than search mechanics.

## Continuation policy quality versus search depth

Ataraxos uses the trained policy for simulated continuation moves rather than solving each future subgame. This is a major simplifying assumption. A shallow search with strong information-safe continuation policies may outperform deeper search with weak or information-leaking policies. As Manabot improves, the optimal balance between policy quality, belief quality, rollout count, depth, and leaf evaluation may change. Measure these dimensions rather than assuming deeper search is always better.

# VII. A scientific framework for future experiments

The following is **guidance for experimental interpretation**, not an execution sequence or set of agent tickets.

### Preserve causal clarity

An experiment should isolate one hypothesized mechanism wherever possible. If a new regularizer, new opponent pool, new search method, and faster simulator are introduced together, a stronger checkpoint does not tell us which idea worked. Where interaction effects are the hypothesis—for example, coordinated regularization and update size—use a factorial comparison that measures those interactions.

### Measure both behavior and outcomes

Every important comparison should consider: (a) optimization diagnostics, (b) action distributions on fixed information-state probes, (c) historical and held-out opponent strength with uncertainty, and (d) total training or inference compute. This helps distinguish real strategic learning from exploitation of a narrow training population.

### Respect information boundaries and game semantics

Managym remains the authority for legality, state transitions, and possible-world semantics. Manabot owns policy learning, belief inference, planning, and evaluation. Search must not expose sampled hidden truth to the acting policy. New fast paths must preserve rules correctness and replay determinism; speedups without semantic equivalence are not meaningful scientific comparisons.

### Treat negative results as evidence

A cyclic schedule that fails, a fixed-reference KL that provides no benefit beyond old-policy KL, or a GPU prototype that loses to multicore CPU are valuable findings. Preserve experiment identity, world/content versions, model and observation/action schemas, seeds, opponents, compute class, confidence intervals, and observed failure modes. Fantasia's existing experiment ledger and world-pinned arena provide the natural foundation.

### Separate what is known from what is conjectured

| Statement | Status |
|---|---|
| Ataraxos achieved unusually strong Stratego results at comparatively low estimated compute cost | Reported empirical result |
| Its chosen annealing, filtering, and search regularization matter within the published configuration | Supported by ablations |
| Precisely coordinated annealing is superior to independently retuned schedules | Not isolated conclusively |
| Cyclic exploration would help Manabot escape strategic traps | Hypothesis |
| KL to the raw network can protect search from overfitting | Supported in Ataraxos; transfer to MTG to test |
| The extra fixed-reference KL improves Manabot search beyond KL to the raw policy | Open experiment |
| CUDA will outperform an optimized multicore Managym end to end | Unknown; requires measurement |
| Belief calibration and information-safe continuations are crucial to robust MTG search | Strong conceptual motivation; empirical magnitude to measure |

# The fundamental questions to keep returning to

**What causes genuine strategic progress?** Not just higher recent win rate, but improvement that persists across opponents, decks, and checkpoints.

**What causes learning to stall, collapse, or cycle?** Can we identify the mechanism from regularization, policy changes, opponent distributions, and interpretable behaviors rather than diagnosing from loss curves alone?

**How should computation be divided between experience, learning, belief inference, and search?** The optimal allocation may change as policy quality improves. More simulation and more search are only useful insofar as they produce reliable strategic improvement.

The long-term aim is not to copy Ataraxos's optimizer or simulator. It is to build a research system that can **observe, explain, and deliberately improve the emergence of superhuman MTG strategy**.
