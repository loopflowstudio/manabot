\begin{abstract}
We are training agents to play \textit{Magic: The Gathering} by self-play. Part~I says what we have demonstrated so far and what our failures taught us. Part~II is a recipe: each component of the system, what it does, the work it draws on, and where we depart from it. Part~II assumes the reader already knows why reinforcement learning for games is hard; Appendix~\ref{app:map} maps those difficulties to the literature for anyone who wants that framing. At small scale, on one fixed matchup, self-play now learns, and the rules engine is no longer a bottleneck. Search-as-teacher did not compound, and no single piece of our adapted Ataraxos recipe has yet shown a benefit on its own. The agents we have played still feel like beginners.
\end{abstract}

\part{What we have demonstrated}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Status}

Manabot trains one network to play both sides of a single matchup, \textsc{UR Lessons} versus \textsc{GW Allies} from the \textit{Avatar: The Last Airbender} set. Training uses self-play from terminal reward with an adaptation of the Ataraxos update \citep{sokota2026ataraxos}. The rules engine, managym, is a deterministic, forkable Rust implementation of a two-player rules subset. The largest run used about 51M learner decisions on one L4 GPU. The agents beat scripted bots that avoid the most obvious blunders. When we have played them ourselves, they feel like beginners. We do not yet have a rating system that compares against human play.

\section{The rules engine}

\paragraph{Rules.} managym implements a two-player subset of the \textit{Comprehensive Rules}: priority, the stack, combat, triggered and activated abilities, a simplified layer system for continuous effects, mid-resolution choices, and state-based actions. Replacement and prevention effects, full layer dependencies and mulligans are absent. Cards are authored as typed programs rather than parsed from Oracle text or hand-coded per card name. A generic interpreter executes them and refuses any program it cannot execute. Rules behavior is pinned by conformance fixtures, and card data is checked against a committed Scryfall snapshot. That check exists because a 2026 audit found 20 of 55 hand-transcribed cards wrong.

\paragraph{Information boundaries.} At each decision the engine gives each player an observation of only what that player can see, plus the legal actions and the objects each one refers to. Viewer-equivalence, replay and leakage checks are hard gates. An early bug let the ordering of a hidden card pool change search results between states that should have looked identical to the player. The observation and action encoding is versioned as a \emph{world}. Checkpoints load only in the world they were trained in, and a nightly learnability check looks for encodings that make two different decisions indistinguishable. The most recent broad search found none.

\paragraph{How the engine got fast, and why it is not on a GPU.} The engine began as a Python prototype. Coding agents translated it into C++, and we then rewrote that C++ by hand. A profiling pass in January 2026 raised C++ throughput from 16k to 71k steps/s. The C++ design used pointer-heavy objects with tangled ownership. In March 2026 we ported to Rust with a data-oriented design: arenas with typed indices, enums with exhaustive matching, and one flat game state, which also makes exact cloning cheap. Vectorization and observation encoding moved into Rust with zero-copy buffers, taking environment-only throughput from 24k to 183k steps/s on an Apple M4 Max. With inference in the loop, throughput fell to 2.0k steps/s, and inference took 97\% of step time. Later batching work raised in-loop inference from 2k to 24.5k observations/s.

In the current profile, policy calls are about 87\% of collection time: 51\% for the opponent and 36\% for the learner. Engine stepping is about 4.5\%. By Amdahl's law, even an infinitely fast engine would save at most about 4.5\% of collection time. \textit{Magic}'s rules are also branchy --- the stack, triggers, variable action lists --- which suits GPUs poorly, and a GPU port would have to re-implement every card. Stratego's fixed board and simple rules made a CUDA simulator a cheap choice for Ataraxos; they do not here. A GPU simulator's main advantage is very large co-located batches. Our calls average only about 11 positions across 64 games, so we pursue the same gain on CPU first: more concurrent games, double-buffering, and merging the learner's and opponent's inference calls. Search changes this calculation. If search returns to training, forking becomes engine work again, and we would re-profile.

\section{Network}

A single network encodes every visible object --- players, cards, permanents --- and mixes them with attention \citep{vaswani2017attention}. Cards enter as tokens of their typed programs, encoded by a GRU, alongside explicit features; the intent is to embed what a card does, not just its name \citep{bertram2024representations}. Each legal action is scored together with the objects it refers to, so casting, targeting, attacking, blocking and paying costs all go through one action head. Unlike a pointer network \citep{vinyals2015pointer}, scoring is per legal action rather than per input position. A value head predicts win, draw or loss. The baseline model is 64 wide with 2 attention layers; at our current horizon the smallest model learned as well as larger ones.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Self-play collection}

Many games step in lockstep, so one inference call serves many positions; the Rust vector environment writes observations into shared buffers without copying. The learner plays both decks and both seats against a current copy of itself, using a cross-balanced matchup schedule. Weights are frozen within each collection batch, but a game may span updates. For every row the collector stores the acting player's observation, the chosen action, and the full collection distribution over legal actions, which the update's KL terms need. Terminal outcomes supply labels, never policy inputs. Opponent actions are stepped between learner decisions and receive no gradient.

Two lessons shaped this. Batching was the largest single speedup: at equal experience, 256 streams trained the baseline model about 20 times faster than 4 streams. And in-loop inference, not the engine, sets the pace; calls still average only about 11 positions across 64 games, because the learner and opponent are separate calls and games wait on one another.

\paragraph{Four ways to limit change.} The learning rate, ratio clipping and the two KL terms all slow the policy down, but they measure different things.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
\textbf{Knob} & \textbf{Measures change in} & \textbf{Relative to} & \textbf{Main job} \\
\midrule
Learning rate & parameters & nothing & optimization speed \\
Ratio clipping & sampled action's probability & $\pi_{\text{old}}$ & cap any one sample's pull \\
$\KL(\pi\,\|\,\pi_{\text{old}})$ & whole action distribution & $\pi_{\text{old}}$ & trust region on this batch \\
$\KL(\pi\,\|\,\rho)$ & whole action distribution & fixed magnet & keep strategic breadth \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Clipping covers only the sampled action. Once a sample's ratio passes $1\pm\epsilon$, its gradient goes to zero, but nothing pulls the policy back if other samples push it further. The KL to $\pi_{\text{old}}$ covers every legal action and acts as a restoring force. Computing it requires the full collection distribution, so the collector stores that distribution for every row. The magnet KL has the same form, but its anchor is fixed: the KL to $\pi_{\text{old}}$ limits speed, and the magnet KL limits where the policy can end up. Our magnet is uniform over action types, then uniform within a type. A spell with dozens of target encodings therefore gets no more prior weight than a single attack. In Stratego, the corresponding reference picks a movable piece uniformly, then one of its moves.

\paragraph{The energy reserve.} The view we took from Ataraxos is that a strong early pull toward a broad reference keeps alternative lines alive long enough for the opponent to teach their value. Annealing it too fast gives quick early gains, then entropy collapse, stalled learning and exploitability. Annealing it too slowly leaves the policy diffuse. % TODO: confirm the paper's own wording for this analogy.
This is not a claim that entropy equals strength; random mana payments raise entropy without strategic mixing. It is a claim about keeping the capacity to adapt. Measuring it needs entropy by decision class, such as $P(\text{pass} \mid \text{instant in hand, mana open})$. Today we log only global entropy from the last minibatch.

\paragraph{Off-policy data.}
\label{sec:offpolicy}
The policy gradient averages over states and actions that the \emph{current} policy would produce. Data from an older policy is wrong in four ways:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{Actions.} They need importance ratios, which have high variance.
\item \textbf{States.} Correcting the state distribution exactly requires ratio products over the whole game. Per-step ratios averaging 1.05 over 300 decisions would multiply to about $2\times10^6$.
\item \textbf{Advantages.} They were computed under the old policy's continuation.
\item \textbf{Opponent.} In self-play the opponent in the data is stale too.
\end{itemize}
Value learning that combines bootstrapping, function approximation and off-policy data can diverge \citep{sutton2018rl}. We keep the policy close to the collecting policy instead of correcting the state distribution: weights are frozen per batch, there is one epoch and no replay buffer, and clipping, the collection KL, gradient-norm clipping and an annealed learning rate bound each update. Collection probabilities are saved per row and checked against the selected-action likelihoods. Advantage filtering deliberately changes which states the policy learns from.

\paragraph{Departures from the source.} Following \texttt{docs/ataraxos.md}: learner decisions replace Stratego's same-player positions; MTG action types replace the piece-then-move reference groups; CPU learner streams replace large GPU simulator batches; and quantile interpolation, Adam constants and the iteration origin were unspecified in the source, so our choices are explicit rather than recovered.

\section{Beliefs and local search}

\paragraph{Why not poker methods.} CFR and its descendants solve for a strategy over every private state a player could hold \citep{zinkevich2007cfr,brown2018libratus}. Public-belief-state methods turn that into a search with well-defined values \citep{moravcik2017deepstack,brown2020rebel,schmid2023sog}. They are the correct theory, but expensive here. Today's opponent hands are poker-sized: 10{,}832 seven-card hands, against 1{,}326 in hold'em. That number grows to about $10^7$ at 40 distinct card names, before library order. Actions are variable and combinatorial, and games last hundreds of decisions. Stratego shares \textit{Magic}'s huge hidden state and long games, and DeepNash and Ataraxos succeeded there with regularized self-play rather than belief solving. That made them the closer precedent.

\paragraph{Search as an inference-time correction.} Ataraxos reverses the KataGo arrangement: self-play teaches the policy, and search makes a small correction at inference. A belief network, trained by supervised learning on the true hidden state from self-play games, samples hidden positions. A frozen policy plays depth-limited rollouts, a value network scores them, and the averaged values $q(a)$ feed one magnetic mirror descent step \citep{sokota2023mmd}:
\[
\pi^+(a) \;\propto\; \pi_0(a)^{\frac{1}{1+\eta\alpha}}\;\rho(a)^{\frac{\eta\alpha}{1+\eta\alpha}}\;\exp\!\Big(\tfrac{\eta}{1+\eta\alpha}\,q(a)\Big).
\]
This is the maximizer of $\sum_a \pi(a)q(a) - \alpha\KL(\pi\|\rho) - \eta^{-1}\KL(\pi\|\pi_0)$. Each KL becomes a log-probability term weighted by its strength, so the update starts from the raw policy $\pi_0$, blends in the magnet $\rho$, and tilts toward higher value. It anchors noisy rollouts to what self-play learned and keeps the policy mixed, where an argmax would not. The paper writes the logits as $(q + \alpha\log\rho + \beta\log\pi_0)/(\alpha+\beta)$ with $\beta=1/\eta$, and reports $\alpha=0.002$, $\beta=0.02$, 1{,}000 rollouts and depth 40. Our defaults are $\alpha=0.02$, $\beta=1$, depth 2 and one sampled world. With these, $q$ is multiplied by about 0.98 in the logits, against about 45 in the paper. Unless our values are on a much larger scale --- which we have not checked --- our search barely moves the policy.

Our continuations act from each simulated player's own observation, and values are averaged across sampled worlds before an action is chosen. The value network sees only the acting player's observation, but is trained on outcomes that depend on the true hidden state. It therefore estimates the value of an information state, averaged over hidden states in proportion to how often self-play produces them. Belief sampling and local search run end to end: 840 held-out sampled hands had no support violations, and 183 search commands replayed with no mismatch. Neither has yet improved on the policy alone. Distilling the \emph{regularized} search distribution back into the policy is not part of Ataraxos; for us it is an open experiment.

\paragraph{Strategy fusion: suspected, not isolated.}
\label{sec:fusion}
Searching sampled hidden worlds as if they were face-up has two known flaws \citep{frank1998bridge,long2010pimc}. \emph{Strategy fusion} arises when the search makes the player's \emph{later} choices inside each sampled world: it credits the player with a different perfect plan in each world, which no real player can follow. A continuation policy that sees only its own player's observation cannot do this. Neither can one-ply search with random rollouts, which is what our flat search used. Separately, if the simulated opponent sees the full state, bluffing is worth nothing in every sampled world. Our July draft attributed the hold-the-wipe failure to strategy fusion. A later review judged that premature: random continuations rarely execute ``wait, then wipe'' even with everything face-up, so rollout pathology is the more likely cause. We designed a two-by-two test --- hidden versus face-up information, crossed with random versus strong continuations --- but have not run it. A preregistered tripwire for fusion, an exploiter reaching at least 65\% against the distilled student, did not fire; the best attacker reached 29.8\%.

\section{Evaluation}

Three kinds of progress are easy to confuse: \emph{optimization} progress (loss, KL, entropy), \emph{relative} progress (wins against the latest opponent), and \emph{durable} progress (strength across past, varied and held-out opponents). The first two can improve while the third gets worse. Our practice follows from this:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{Balanced play.} Every evaluation balances seats and decks, because between uniformly random players on a creature deck, going first wins 94\% of games.
\item \textbf{Head-to-head Tournaments.} A Tournament file names a roster of checkpoints and fixed opponents, and plays every pairing on the same held-out deals with both decks and both seats. This separates checkpoints that look the same against a scripted opponent. The arena fits ratings across the whole population.
\item \textbf{Anchors.} Random and scripted-greedy anchors are saturating. We are testing whether a rule-based heuristic bot and frozen trained checkpoints can rank checkpoints of different strength.
\item \textbf{Replay and seeds.} Every game is replay-verified. Claims about a training method use independent training seeds, because game-level intervals only measure one checkpoint's evaluation noise.
\item \textbf{Preregistration.} The question, numeric prediction, kill criteria and cost cap are committed before the run. Refuted predictions stay on the record.
\end{itemize}

\section{Open questions}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Curriculum.} Which matchups (mirrors or cross-deck), which opponents, how many decks? The energy reserve keeps options alive; the curriculum decides which experiences get to use them. Ataraxos did little curriculum design.
\item \textbf{The magnet.} Uniform over action types, uniform over complete actions, a heuristic bot, or a human prior? What test shows that it improves robustness, not just entropy?
\item \textbf{Do the pieces only matter together, or only at scale?} How can we tell cheaply?
\item \textbf{Exploration.} Is a missing behavior unexplored, unrepresentable, or unrewarded by current opponents?
\item \textbf{Search.} Does inference-time search help a strong policy here, and is distilling regularized search targets the KataGo move for \textit{Magic}, or a return of the label-cost problem?
\item \textbf{Representation.} Which auxiliary targets improve strength per environment step, and do typed card programs transfer to held-out cards?
\item \textbf{Evaluation.} What evidence would show a stronger mixed strategy, not a better response to one evaluation set, and how do we compare against humans?
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations}

The rules subset omits replacement and prevention effects, full layers and mulligans. The card pool is two decks. Opponents are scripted bots and our own checkpoints; there is no human evaluation. Several self-play findings rest on two or three training seeds. The literature mapping reflects our reading and is not a survey.
